PREFACE OF THE EDITOR

THE object of the following little work, and of any
others of the like sort which it may be permitted to the
Editor to publish, is to meet, as far as may be, some of
the wants which the mighty stirring of minds within our
Church for some time past has created. Such stirrings
always leave something to be supplied. God mostly sets
the heart in motion, makes her feel her want of somewhat
out of herself and beyond all created beings, her need of
Himself, Who alone can fill her ; but how to attain to
Him, He leaves most often to the guidance of others.
Having brought her to the Holy City, He withdraws
His star for a time, and leaves it to the Church to point
her to Bethlehem ; although He will ever accompany her
on the way, and His light will in the end stream on the
place where she shall find Whom she seeks, the Living
Bread, Who came down from heaven, and she shall know
the Object of her search, as well by hidden and heavenly
tokens made known to herself alone, as by the teaching
of the Church out of the Holy Scriptures.
In the present time there is a craving after a higher
life ; stricter and more abiding penitence ; deeper and fuller
a3

devotion ; mental prayer ; meditation upon God and His
Holy Mysteries ; more inward love to Him ; oneness of
will with Him in all things ; more habitual recollection in
Him amid the duties of daily life ; entire consecration to
God; deadness to self and to the world ; growth in the
several Christian graces in detail ; self-knowledge in
order to victory over self ; daily strife ; stricter con-formity with our Lord's blessed Commandments and all-holy Life, sympathy with His Passion, " the fellowship of
His sufferings," oneness with Him. Yet in all, people
feel that they lack instruction ; they see dimly what God
would have of them, they see not how to set about it.
They see themselves to be set in a certain direction, and
that their path would lead heavenwards, and that God
has placed them, however unworthy, in it ; but they are
amazed, often, at the very newness of the blessed pro-spect before them ; they long to do something for Him
Who has done so much for them , but if they ask,
" Lord, what wouldest Thou have me to do ? " the
Voice does not now answer, " Arise and go into the city,
and it shall be told thee, what thou must do." The evil
habits of a past century have well-nigh cut off from pas-toral intercourse the very classes which are now perhaps
most deeply stirred, those most highly educated ; they are " gathered," as was promised, " one by one,"” " one in
a city and two in a family," as His gracious Voice sum-moneth, now this one, then that one, to arise, gird them-selves , and follow Him. The very method of His calling
for the time insulates them : " the Spirit bloweth where
It listeth, and thou knowest the sound thereof, but canst
not tell whence It cometh or whither It goeth." It has

come, is gone, and those around have not as yet felt Its
I new force. They " hear not the Voice," which speaketh
to the inmost heart. The kingdom of God is set up
" within," and " cometh not with observation." They to
whom it comes, alone " see that great" sight, and they are
" left alone." (Dan. x. 7.8.) Precisely in the case ofthose,
-the tenderest, the most scrupulous, most conscientious,
-whom, both from age and sex, God has formed to
lean, and whom of late He has drawn very wonderfully,
forms of society interfere with free consultation of spi-ritual guides, even where it might be had; deep feeling
at all times shows itself unwillingly ; it shrinks from
attracting attention by what is unusual ; the more, if those
near it, would not sympathise, or would wonder at it.
Struggle with difficulty seems an appointed portion of
the trial of its reality ; visibly it finds no human sym-pathy, that it may lean the more on Him, Who can
Alone wholly sympathise with the hearts which He
made, and which He took, to bear their iniquities and to
heal them. Our very Church has not yet adapted her-self to the new wants of her children. Services, which
would meet and calm those wants, and provide a home
forweary, aching, anxious hearts, yea, wherein they might
pour themselves out, in sanctioned words, to a Parent's
Heart, yea, rest themselves on a Father's Bosom, or have
the nearness of a Saviour's love and His very Presence,
are still withheld or are rare. Duties which our Church
authoritatively enjoins, her living voice all but disavows;
so that to obey her seems almost an act of individual
will; nay, it might or would be, were there not a higher
and controlling Will which constrains them, a "Voice"

which they " hear behind them," saying, " This is the
way, walk ye in it. " They who are first called are saved
the greater perils ofpopularity ; they have the special diffi-culties of apparent isolation and want of guidance. They
wish, perhaps, to observe holy seasons, of penitence or of
joy, and amid (too often) closed Churches, they have
all to do for themselves, and know not wherewith to
occupy themselves ; how they may gain the fruits of the
season ; wherewith to begin. They would meditate on
our Blessed Lord's Sacred Life and Passion, and know not
how to fix their thoughts ; they see that the standard of
Christian graces in Holy Scripture is far above what
seems even thought of, and know not how to take the
first step ; they feel their own hearts to be a very laby-rinth, hidden and perplexed to themselves, and know not
how to disentangle its mazes. It must, indeed, have
impressed itself upon every one who has been admitted
to witness such cases, how God, in the end, turneth all
the perplexities and disquietudes of the souls whom He
calls, to their endless good, teaching them to take the
stricter, because, amid doubt, the safer line ; to use,
under His own holy and immediate Teaching, greater
severity with themselves, than unholy man would have
ventured to suggest ; drawing them the more closely to
Himself, through the fear lest unconsciously they part
from Him. Untaught by man, they are far more taught
by Him, the promised, the Only Teacher, Who also
Alone teacheth in all through whom He teacheth .

guidance in the habits of meditation and mental prayer,
to self-knowledge, to penitence, the spiritual life, the
bearing of His Cross and conformity to Him, holy per-formance of the ordinary actions of daily life, Divine love,
enlarged and deeper views of the Christian graces, were

Then as to that other class, our very habits as a
nation render contemplation peculiarly difficult. Every
thing among us has a practical uncontemplative direction.
Everydepartment of knowledge partakes of our tendency.
We seem to study nothing for its own sake, never to go
back to first principles, or to view any thing in the
abstract. All, every where, is busy, active, hurried life.
We have, as yet, no places of retreat for " persons of
mortified tempers," such as Archbishop Leighton sighed
for ; we have well-nigh no seasons for it. Except perhaps
during the most solemn part of Lent, or Passion-week,
even religious persons have mostly no thought of retiring

a whole day, for humiliation, consideration of themselves

without ourselves, is our only measure of excellence.
Thedaily service is considered as the least part ofduty,
because it does not directly bear upon ministerial activity;
in it we do nothing but pray to, praise, listen to God, and
so it is extensively thought to be that part of the office
of our very Clergy most readily dispensed with. With
many religious persons it would probably be an effort
to fix the mind for one quarter of an hour upon God in
contemplation or mental prayer. Our recent Theology
has been also adverse to it; for it has almost lost the
very idea of contemplating God in Himself, as He has
revealed Himself, the Everblessed Trinity in Unity, and
Unity in Trinity. The developments of the Athanasian
Creed against heresies appeared to very many, it must
be confessed, abstract and apart from themselves. Divines
had well-nigh established it as a principle, that we knew

nothing of God, except in His direct relation to our-selves. It may have been well, that people left off speak-ing of God, except in a vague way, lest, if they had
attempted more, they should have fallen unconsciously
into heresy, which they so little feared, and whose lan-guage has been used without suspicion.
It must be owned also, that our writers have, for some
time at least, or for the most part, drawn too much from
their own resources. The richest and most thoughtful
of our writers, such as Hooker, and Andrewes, and
Bp. Taylor, are precisely those who have most largely
converted into the substance of their own minds the
thoughts of the saints and doctors who have been before.
Theyhave not only produced, but re-produced ; like the
scribe instructed to the kingdom of heaven, bringing
forth out of his treasure things new and old. Yet, in the
main, and of late certainly, we seem to have been cut off
from intercourse with those before as well as around us.
We have been severed from the ancient hills on which
the Sun of Righteousnes saforetime rested, and which
He illumined, as well as from the plain country round
about us.
Yet it was never meant that any portion of the Chris-tian Church should be thus insulated. What is given by
His good Spirit, at least what He has preserved, is not
for one set of men or one nation only. Whatever He
has preserved, He has preserved for our use ; whatever
He has any where given to the Church, He has given to
the whole Church. It belongs to us, as a portion of that
Church. It were an unthankful neglect of His gift, thus
to think ourselves self-sufficing, as though each national

First, then, as to doctrine ;-
Ill-informed as is that dread of the mention of the
value of good works, so intense at the present time, still,
whenever it is sincere, and not the result of mere theory,
one must respect it for its object's sake. Distressing as
it often is, and unquestionably as it has crippled, and
does cripple, high and devoted service, still, since it is of
a mixed character, and allowed by the Providence of
Almighty God, one may hope that it may be overruled
to some providential purpose in this restoration of our
Church. The better and more earnest Pharisaism must
have some deep hold on our nature, since, before his
conversion, it could hold such an one as St. Paul. And
although, for the most part, those alone will rest unduly
upon works who have but few, and the very thought
were inconsistent with any high degree of attainment, of
which humility is the very groundwork, and real saints
have ever been so wholly and inwardly penetrated by
humility, as thereby even to become unintelligible to
common men, still, in our state of transition, nothing
which casts us back upon and into ourselves can fail of
its use. They who have been led to a deeper and truer
system, cannot have too vividly before their eyes any
perils which may lie near it. Shocking as the doubt
implied is, the warnings to look to Him Alone, in
Whom our life is, may promote at once humility and
love.
Still, it must be said, that this very dread does itself
rest upon a defective system, and often implies a lurking
Pelagianism. For why dread the mention of works, if
men realized that " God wrought all our works for us ? "

Holy Scripture says, " Now if thou hast received, why
dost thou boast thyself, as though thou hadst not re-ceived ? " If, then, we are vividly conscious, that of our-selves we were and are nothing, that we have been taken
out of ourselves, that all which there may be of good in
any we have " received," we could not boast. To think,
then, that the acknowledgment of the value of good
works would lead to self-exaltation, implies that they are
thought to be a man's own works, not the gifts of God.
The Ancient Church could fearlessly acknowledge them,
because, according to the deep saying of S. Augustine, it
believed that " Deus in nobis dona coronat sua," " God
crowneth in us His own gifts ;" " His own gifts," but " in
us. " Whatever we are, whencesoever we came,-peni-tents, restored, defiled, pure, with the deep scars of
still-repented sin, or with the fresh purity of Baptismal
innocence, on the first step of that ladder whose summit
is at the throne of God, or at the last, just forsaking
earth, or all but attained to Heaven, she regarded us
(so that we be not reprobates) as " in Christ." His we
are by purchase ; His by possession ; His by our engraft-ing into Him ; His by His indwelling Spirit ; His by
having been made one body with Him ; having passed
into Him, received His Image and Likeness, His Death,
Burial, and Resurrection ; He the Head, we the mem-bers ; He the Vine, we the branches ; He our Light, our
Life, our Salvation, our Righteousness, our Sanctification,
our Redemption. How should we be any thing of our-selves, have any thing of ourselves, since we have ceased,
in amanner, to be ourselves ? The outward man is, day
by day, decaying and being cast away ; our inward man

hath its birth, growth, renewal, support, increase, perfect-ing, cleansing of its imperfections, final acceptance, from
Him. His gracious look it was which recalled us to our-selves from vanities in which we were straying ; the
melting of our icy hearts was that " He sendeth out
His Word and melteth them ; He bloweth with His
Breath, and the waters flow." His Hand re-made
us when we had unmade ourselves what He had again
made us ; or if any were never such as to need an entire
conversion, “ from Thee it was that they should not so
be, as from Thee it was that those who have been, should
not ever so be ; from Thee it was, that both might
know from Whom it was 1. " " The Cords of His Passion
drew us, His Crown of Thorns fenced our way, His
Nails held us, His Cross sustained us ;" yea, " His Soul
sanctifieth us, His Body strengthens us, His Blood re-deemeth us, the Water from His Side cleanses us, His
Bruises heal us, His Bloody Sweat refreshes us, His
Wounded Side hides us 2 :" in It, as in a secret Chamber,
the Hiding-place of His Presence, we may hide us, with
Him die, with Him be buried, with Him abide, " until the
tyranny" of the evil world, and " the indignation be over-past," and we, having " died with Him" and " slept in
Him," " awake up after His likeness and are satisfied
with It. " If we be Christ's, all which is His is ours ;
we are all His.

There are but two ways of viewing all things, in and
out of Christ. All short of either is imperfect and in-consistent in good or ill. Without Him every thing is
dead ; in Him every thing may live. Forms and rites,
the beauty of holiness and of worship, would be an idle
pageant, unless they speak of Him ; alms-deeds were
natural kindliness ; fasting, "an image of famine ;" en-durance, Stoicism ; penitential tears, the sorrow of Esau ;
self- affliction, self-condemnation ; out of Him: in Him,

Actions of this sort have been indefinitely multiplied :

the sort, which yet have been found useful in forming

unsuspected by men, they will lead on to others, and may
be forming in us that " broken and contrite heart which
Godwill not despise ;" only that in the feeling of our
own unworthiness, we must ever keep His Cross in sight,
and " out of the deep" of our misery, " call to the deep"
of His mercy.
Such acts as these are, of course, given as specimens
only to beginners ; they who are more advanced will be
guided by Him Who has led them thus far ; it need
hardly be said that such as the writer would not suggest
a rule. There is again no end of the variety of the ways

In the very contemplation of those awful sufferings,
there will probably be a difference between the mode in
which most recent foreign writers dwell upon them, and
our own, arising, it is to be feared, from our want of the
habit of meditation. The foreign writers dwell (some-times, it seems, almost too exclusively) on the visible
Sufferings of our Lord ; the habit of most of us has pro-bably been to think only of His bearing the wrath of the
Father. Yet both have been made known to us ; and
the very fulness of the Gospels in dwelling upon those

Sufferings, is a plain indication to us how we ought to
meditate upon them. Even where circumstances are not
actually told, since, if we think of them at all, we must
think of them as taking place in some way, we cannot
help, in our own minds, supplying them ; and we may ac-cept thankfully any suggestions which enable us to supply

Right Hand graciously, when laid upon the Cross, it seems
scarcely an addition to Holy Scripture ; it harmonizes
with it and His Divine Meekness. So, again, with regard
to what is so much dwelt upon, the Effusion of His
Sacred Blood ; it is involved in what Holy Scripture
tells us. The disfiguring of His Form is the most literal
fulfilment of the prophecy of Isaiah. (Ch. liii.) On the
other hand, we must be careful, while dwelling upon His
Bodily Sufferings, to keep in mind those which we can
still less understand, and which foreign writers seem
sometimes on the verge of passing over. Thus the pre-sent writer, in speaking of the words, " I thirst," although
he quotes the prophecy of which these words were the
fulfilment, almost seems, in his wish to set those Suffer-ings before us, to forget that the words were said in order
to fulfil that prophecy -to represent them as too much
direct and natural results of those Sufferings. There
seems a risk, in a word, lest such representations become
too human. But our tendency is all the other way ; and
this is said, as well that we may not take offence at what
we have not, as that we may not part with what we have.
We may learn much, if we learn to dwell not simply on
the doctrine of the Atonement in the abstract, but on

our Lord, living and dying for us ; to have vividly before
us the whole series of His Humiliations, and Shame, and
extreme Sufferings, of which the Cross was the com-pletion, and in this lengthened contemplation to behold
our sins. It is to be feared that, while we preach the
doctrine of Justification, we preach, much less than is sup-posed, Christ Crucified, or our Crucifixion with Him ;
that we fall very short of that preaching, which much-quoted Scripture words suggest : " before whose eyes
Jesus Christ has been evidently set forth, crucified among
you ; " and which a reverent contemplation of the history
of the Gospels would supply¹. And so the affections of
our people are often left so cold and dead.
