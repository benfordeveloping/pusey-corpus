The Meditations of S. Anselm were his own com- 
munings with God, in which he poured out his heart 
before Him. His disciple Eadmer, the compa- 
panion of his toils and of his exile, says of them ; 
" What a of his vigils ? The whole day most often 
did not suffice for giving counsel ; the greater part 
of the night was wont to be added to this end. 
Moreover by night he corrected books, which up 
to that time had throughout the world been most 
corrupt ; he gave himself continuously to holy medi- 
tations, and from the contemplation of tlie sovereign 
bliss and longing for life eternal, he poured forth 
showers of tears. He wept most bitterly the mi- 
series of this life and his own sins, (whatever he 
may have had,) and the sins of others, and scarcely 
took a little sleep before the night- vigils and often 
none." 

Of the Prayers, on the contrary, Eadmer speaks 
generally, as having been written and published in 
compliance with the desire of friends. S. Anselm 
himself mentions four prayers, and four only, as 
having been written at the request of others.**

The good service which Dean Stanhope did in lay- 
ing open to the English mind the fervid language of 
these devotional writers, is not lessened by his having 
mixed overmuch of water with the good wine, in con- 
formity with the taste of his day. 

Besides the large insertions from S. Anselm in the 
Meditations and the Manual and those in the work 
" on loving God, " another of the compilations of that 
time, ** de contridone cordis" was made up mostly of 
passages of S. Anselm, which seemed to the compiler 
most suited to move men to " contrition of heart." 
These selections were in their own nature anonymous, 
and S. Anselm, thus, long formed to piety minds who 
knew not his name, nor to whom they owed their 
blessings. 

When printing gave larger circulation to books, 
five-sixths of the works attributed to S. Anselm were 
not his. Of these Meditations and Prayers, two only 
(Meditation ii. and Prayer x.) were published, as 
his, before 1573 ; nearly sixty years more only 
added two, the second and the ninth. The rest were 
first published by Theophl. Raynaud, in 1630.

The condensed style of the original has been re- 
tained as far as possible in the translation. And this, 
because the very condensation of fervent thoughts 
gives them the more power in stirring the thoughts of 
others. This is the especial office of private medita- 
tions and prayers. No words can fully represent the 
wants of anoflier. Even the fervid thoughts of a de- 
vout writer must be filled up, expanded, applied, 
by the soul itself. Whatever human helps may be 
given it, prayer must, in the end, be the voice or 
thoughts, or mute longings of the soul itself to God. 
Even the deep words of our Lord's own prayer, must, 
as it ascends to God, within the compass of its seven 
petitions, bear up as many varied longings as there 
are souls which utter it. The more condensed the 
prayer, the more it invites, the more capable it is of 
this varied use. T&e deepest prayers are the most 
suggestive. They not only mould our thoughts by 
their words, but they give rise to other words and 
thoughts ; and God by them, lifls us up above the 
ground and sets us above ourselves, whence, so to 
speak, we may spring upwards to Him. No use, 
however attentive, of the words of others, can take 
the place of the aspiration of the soul itself, drawn by 
God, to God. Any one, however unaccustomed to 
mental prayer, should, at least, interpose pauses in 
the use of vocal prayer, wherein, himself to speak 
to God, to listen to God. In this way, the prayers 
of others pass, as the Psalms do by habitual use, 
into the substance of our own souls. They become 
our own words, and recur to us whenever we use 
them, full of all the thoughts which God has here- 
tofore given us through them. 

May God, through these prayers, kindle in us a 
hatred of sin, lowly thoughts of ourselves, adoring, 
reverential love of Himself ; strengthen us against 
temptation, lift us above the world, give us perse-
